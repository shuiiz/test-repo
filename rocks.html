All About Rocks

Rocks are the solid mineral materials that make up Earth's crust. Geologists classify all rocks into three main groups based on how they form: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic. Each type has its own story of creation, distinct physical properties, and a rich variety of specimens found around the world.

Rock Types Overview

The rock cycle (the continuous process by which rocks transform from one type to another over geological time) connects all three rock groups. Molten rock cools to form igneous rocks; erosion and deposition create sedimentary layers; and heat and pressure deep in the Earth transform existing rocks into metamorphic ones. Understanding these processes helps explain the extraordinary diversity of rocks found on every continent.

Igneous Rocks

Definition

Igneous rocks are formed when magma (molten rock beneath Earth's surface) or lava (molten rock that has reached the surface through volcanic activity) cools and solidifies. The word "igneous" comes from the Latin ignis, meaning fire.

Formation

Igneous rocks are divided into two main categories based on where they cool. Intrusive igneous rocks (also called plutonic rocks) form deep underground when magma cools slowly over thousands or millions of years. The slow cooling allows large mineral crystals to grow, giving these rocks a coarse-grained texture. Extrusive igneous rocks (also called volcanic rocks) form when lava erupts onto Earth's surface and cools rapidly in air or water. Fast cooling produces small crystals or even a glassy texture with no visible crystals.

Characteristics

Igneous rocks are generally hard and dense. Intrusive types tend to have visible, interlocking crystals in a speckled or mottled pattern. Extrusive types are typically fine-grained or glassy. They usually do not show stratification (distinct layers), which distinguishes them from sedimentary rocks. Many igneous rocks are resistant to weathering and erosion, making them common in mountain ranges and outcrops.

Examples

  • Granite — A coarse-grained intrusive rock composed mainly of quartz, feldspar, and mica; very common in continental crust and widely used as a building material.
  • Basalt — A fine-grained extrusive rock; the most common rock type on Earth's ocean floors and a major component of volcanic islands like Hawaii.
  • Obsidian — A naturally occurring volcanic glass formed when lava cools extremely quickly; typically jet black and with a glassy, conchoidal fracture.
  • Pumice — A highly porous, lightweight extrusive rock formed from frothy lava; so light it can float on water.
  • Rhyolite — A fine-grained extrusive rock with a composition similar to granite; often shows banding or flow patterns from its volcanic origin.

Sedimentary Rocks

Definition

Sedimentary rocks are formed from sediment (particles of rock, mineral grains, or organic material) that accumulates in layers and is compressed over time. They make up roughly 75% of the rocks visible at Earth's surface, even though they constitute only about 5% of the crust by volume.

Formation

The formation process begins with weathering — the physical and chemical breakdown of existing rocks — and erosion — the transport of those fragments by wind, water, or ice. Sediment settles in layers called strata at the bottom of rivers, lakes, and seas. Over millions of years, the weight of overlying material compresses the sediment (compaction), and minerals dissolved in groundwater cement the grains together (cementation), turning loose sediment into solid rock through a process called lithification.

Characteristics

The most distinctive feature of sedimentary rocks is their layered appearance. These layers, or beds, often vary in colour, grain size, and composition, recording changes in ancient environments over time. Sedimentary rocks frequently contain fossils (preserved remains of ancient organisms), making them invaluable for understanding Earth's biological history. They tend to be softer and less dense than igneous or metamorphic rocks.

Examples

  • Sandstone — Composed of sand-sized grains of quartz or feldspar cemented together; commonly red, orange, or tan; used widely in construction.
  • Limestone — Formed primarily from the shells and skeletons of marine organisms; mainly composed of calcium carbonate; often contains fossils.
  • Shale — Made from compacted clay or mud; the most abundant sedimentary rock type; fine-grained and tends to split into thin sheets.
  • Conglomerate — Contains rounded pebbles and larger clasts cemented in a fine-grained matrix; indicates a high-energy ancient environment such as a riverbed.
  • Coal — An organic sedimentary rock formed from compressed plant material over millions of years; a major fossil fuel.

Metamorphic Rocks

Definition

Metamorphic rocks are existing rocks — igneous, sedimentary, or older metamorphic — that have been physically and chemically changed by intense heat, pressure, or both, without actually melting. The word "metamorphic" comes from the Greek for "change of form."

Formation

Metamorphism (the transformation process) occurs most commonly deep within the Earth's crust at convergent tectonic plate boundaries, where rocks are buried under great depths or squeezed between colliding plates. The heat and pressure cause minerals within the rock to recrystallise into new forms — often larger crystals aligned in a preferred direction — without the rock ever becoming fully molten. Contact metamorphism happens when rocks are heated by proximity to a magma intrusion, while regional metamorphism affects large areas of crust over millions of years.

Characteristics

A common characteristic of metamorphic rocks is foliation (the alignment of platy minerals such as mica into parallel bands or sheets, giving the rock a layered or banded appearance). Not all metamorphic rocks are foliated — those formed from non-platy minerals, such as quartzite and marble, tend to be non-foliated and have a granular texture. Metamorphic rocks are generally very hard and resistant to erosion.

Examples

  • Marble — Formed when limestone is metamorphosed by heat and pressure; composed of recrystallised calcite; prized for sculpture and architecture.
  • Slate — A fine-grained, strongly foliated rock formed from shale; splits easily into flat sheets; historically used for roofing tiles and blackboards.
  • Quartzite — Formed from sandstone subjected to high heat and pressure; extremely hard and resistant to weathering; used in road construction.
  • Schist — A medium- to coarse-grained foliated rock with visible mica flakes; often has a glittery, silvery appearance.
  • Gneiss — A high-grade metamorphic rock with pronounced banding of light and dark minerals; often forms the cores of ancient mountain ranges.
